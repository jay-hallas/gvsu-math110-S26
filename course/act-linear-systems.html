    Subsection Systems of Linear Equations

    Worksheet Activity: Solving Systems of Linear Equations
    

    A system of linear equations is a pair of equations considered together. The solution is any point \((x, y)\) that satisfies both equations; geometrically, it is where the two lines intersect.
    🔗
    There are three possible outcomes:
    • One solution: the lines intersect at exactly one point. Write the solution as an ordered pair \((x, y)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • No solution: the lines are parallel and never meet. The system is inconsistent.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Infinitely many solutions: the lines are identical and overlap completely.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    For each problem: (a) match each line in the graph to one of the two equations given, (b) identify the system type (one solution, no solution, or infinite solutions), and (c) state the solution if one exists.
    🔗

    Worksheet Activity: Modeling Real-World Scenarios with Linear Systems
    

    For each scenario below, work through the following steps in order.
    1. Define your variables clearly.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Write a system of two linear equations that fits the situation.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Graph both equations in Desmos to find their intersection.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Sketch your graph on the provided grid; choose your own scale, label both axes, and mark the intersection point.
      🔗
      🔗
    5. State the solution, explain what it means in context, and verify it satisfies both equations.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Worked Example. A store sells small candles for $4 each and large candles for $9 each. A customer buys a total of 8 candles and spends exactly $42. How many of each size did they buy?
    🔗
    Let \(x\) = number of small candles purchased. Let \(y\) = number of large candles purchased.
    🔗
    What it tracks Equation
    Total candles bought \(x + y = 8\)
    Total amount spent \(4x + 9y = 42\)
    The intersection is at \((x, y) = (6, 2)\text{,}\) so the customer bought 6 small candles and 2 large candles.
    🔗
    Check: \(6 + 2 = 8\) ✓ and \(4(6) + 9(2) = 42\) ✓
    🔗
    Note: as long as you define your variables consistently, it is fine if you assigned \(x\) and \(y\) the other way around. Your intersection point will have the coordinates swapped, but your conclusion will be equivalent.
    🔗
